Limits & Continuity

On the behavior of functions as they approach — but never quite reach

The concept of a limit is the bedrock upon which all of calculus is constructed. Before we can speak of instantaneous rates of change or the areas under curves, we must first develop a precise language for describing what happens to a function as its input draws arbitrarily close to some target value — even when the function itself is undefined at that very point.

Consider a function f(x) that is defined near a point a, though not necessarily at a itself. We write

Definition — Limit limx → a f(x) = L

if for every ε > 0 there exists a δ > 0 such that
0 < |x − a| < δ ⟹ |f(x) − L| < ε

This ε–δ definition, first rigorously formulated by Weierstrass in the nineteenth century, replaced centuries of vague intuitions about "infinitely small quantities" with an airtight logical framework. It says, in essence: we can make f(x) as close as we like to L, provided we are willing to restrict x to a sufficiently small neighbourhood of a.

"The limit is the ghost of a departed quantity — the shadow that a function casts as it passes near a point."

One-Sided Limits

Not all approaches are symmetric. We may wish to examine the behaviour of f(x) as x approaches a exclusively from the left (values less than a) or from the right (values greater than a). These are denoted limx→a⁻ f(x) and limx→a⁺ f(x) respectively. The two-sided limit exists if and only if both one-sided limits exist and agree.

Continuity

A function f is continuous at a point a if three conditions hold: f(a) is defined, limx→a f(x) exists, and the two are equal. Intuitively, the graph of a continuous function can be drawn without lifting pen from paper — no jumps, no holes, no sudden leaps to infinity.

Historical Note

The idea of limits traces back to Archimedes' method of exhaustion (c. 250 BCE), where he approximated π by inscribing polygons with ever more sides inside a circle. Newton and Leibniz later built calculus on intuitive notions of "fluxions" and "infinitesimals," but it was not until Cauchy and Weierstrass in the 1800s that the ε–δ definition provided the logical foundation we use today.

Fig. 1. The function f(x) = (x²−1)/(x−1) has a removable discontinuity at x = 1. The limit as x → 1 equals 2.

Key Theorem

Squeeze Theorem: If g(x) ≤ f(x) ≤ h(x) near a, and both g and h approach L as x → a, then f must also approach L.

The Derivative

Measuring the instantaneous — from average slopes to a single moment

If limits provide the language of calculus, then the derivative is its first great protagonist. The problem it solves is ancient and practical: given a curve, how steep is it at a particular point? The slope between two points on a line is straightforward, but a curve has no single slope — its steepness changes from point to point.

The derivative resolves this by taking the slope of the secant line through two points on the curve and then shrinking the distance between those points to zero. Formally:

Definition — Derivative f′(a) = limh→0 [f(a + h) − f(a)] / h

Geometrically, f′(a) is the slope of the tangent line to the curve y = f(x) at the point (a, f(a)). Physically, if f(t) gives position as a function of time, then f′(t) gives instantaneous velocity. This single idea — the limit of a ratio of changes — unlocks an enormous range of applications.

Differentiability Implies Continuity

If f is differentiable at a, then f is necessarily continuous at a. The converse, however, is false: the absolute value function f(x) = |x| is continuous everywhere but fails to be differentiable at x = 0, where the graph has a sharp corner. Differentiability is the stronger condition — it requires not just that the function has no gaps, but that it has no kinks.

Fig. 2. The tangent line at a point is the limiting position of secant lines as the second point approaches the first.

"The derivative is to the tangent what the limit is to the approach — the precise capture of a tendency."

Notation

Leibniz notation dy/dx evokes the ratio of infinitesimals. Newton's dot notation ẏ is preferred in physics. Lagrange's f′(x) is compact. All three describe the same object — the instantaneous rate of change.

Differentiation Rules

The algebraic toolkit — computing derivatives without limits

The Power Rule

Power Rule d/dx [xⁿ] = n·xⁿ⁻¹

For any real number n, the derivative of x raised to the n-th power is n times x raised to the (n−1)-th power. This single rule, applied repeatedly, handles all polynomial functions.

The Constant Multiple Rule

d/dx [c·f(x)] = c·f′(x)

Constants pass through the derivative operator unchanged. Scaling a function scales its rate of change by the same factor.

Product & Quotient Rules

Product Rule d/dx [f·g] = f′g + fg′
Quotient Rule d/dx [f/g] = (f′g − fg′) / g²

The derivative of a product is not the product of the derivatives — a common error. The product rule adds two terms, each pairing one function's derivative with the other's original value. The quotient rule follows from the product rule applied to f · g⁻¹.

The Chain Rule

Chain Rule d/dx [f(g(x))] = f′(g(x)) · g′(x)

To differentiate a composite function, differentiate the outer function (evaluated at the inner) and multiply by the derivative of the inner. This is arguably the most powerful rule in the toolkit — it is the mechanism by which calculus handles nested dependencies.

Common Derivatives

d/dx [sin x] = cos x
d/dx [cos x] = −sin x
d/dx [tan x] = sec²x
d/dx [eˣ] = eˣ
d/dx [ln x] = 1/x
d/dx [aˣ] = aˣ ln a

Implicit Differentiation

When y is defined implicitly by an equation involving both x and y (e.g., x² + y² = 25), differentiate both sides with respect to x, treating y as a function of x, and solve for dy/dx.

Applications of Derivatives

From abstract symbols to real-world insight

The derivative's power lies not in its definition but in its applications. With the ability to compute instantaneous rates of change, we can solve problems that would otherwise be intractable: finding the maximum profit, the shortest path, the fastest descent, the point of diminishing returns.

Related Rates

When two or more quantities change with respect to time and are linked by an equation, their rates of change are also linked. By differentiating the relating equation with respect to time, we obtain a relationship among the rates — a technique known as related rates.

Example — Expanding Circle A = πr²
dA/dt = 2πr · dr/dt

If the radius of a circle grows at 3 cm/s, then at the moment when r = 5 cm, the area grows at dA/dt = 2π(5)(3) = 30π cm²/s.

Linear Approximation

Near a point a, a differentiable function is well-approximated by its tangent line:

f(x) ≈ f(a) + f′(a)(x − a)

This is the foundation of linearization and, ultimately, of Taylor series — the idea that smooth functions can be approximated by polynomials of increasing degree.

Optimization

To find the maximum or minimum of a continuous function on a closed interval, evaluate the function at all critical points (where f′ = 0 or f′ is undefined) and at the endpoints. The largest and smallest of these values are the absolute maximum and minimum.

Fig. 3. Critical points and endpoints are candidates for absolute extrema on a closed interval.

Curve Sketching

The first derivative reveals where a function increases (f′ > 0) and decreases (f′ < 0). The second derivative reveals concavity: f″ > 0 means concave up (a cup), f″ < 0 means concave down (a cap). Points where concavity changes are inflection points. Together, these tools let us sketch any function's essential shape.

Integration

Accumulation, area, and the inverse of differentiation

Where the derivative breaks a function into infinitesimal differences, the integral reassembles them. The definite integral of f from a to b computes the signed area between the curve y = f(x) and the x-axis — a quantity that arises naturally in physics (work, displacement, mass) and probability (cumulative distributions).

The formal definition uses Riemann sums: partition [a, b] into n subintervals, choose sample points, form the sum of f(xᵢ*)·Δx, and take the limit as the mesh size approaches zero:

Definition — Definite Integral ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = limn→∞ Σi=1n f(xᵢ*) · Δx

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

This theorem is the bridge between differentiation and integration — the revelation that they are inverse processes.

FTC — Part I If F(x) = ∫ₐˣ f(t) dt, then F′(x) = f(x)
FTC — Part II ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a)

Part I says that the accumulation function built from f has f as its derivative. Part II says that to evaluate a definite integral, find any antiderivative F and take the difference of its values at the endpoints. This transforms the laborious limit of Riemann sums into a problem of finding antiderivatives — a far more tractable task.

Fig. 4. The definite integral as the limit of Riemann sums — the area under the curve, approximated by rectangles of diminishing width.

"Integration is the summation of the infinitely small — the whole recovered from its parts."

Indefinite Integrals

The indefinite integral ∫ f(x) dx = F(x) + C represents the family of all antiderivatives of f. The arbitrary constant C reflects the fact that derivatives erase constant information — infinitely many functions share the same derivative.
